I am a sociologist of stratification studying how social status, educational prestige, educational inequality, and social mobility are jointly produced. My research engages with mainstream discourse and fundamental scientific questions of the role of higher education in alleviating and perpetuating social inequality and mobility. My dissertation addresses the social dimensions of college using traditional quantitative and statistical analyses, survey experimental designs, network analyses, and computational methods for large-scale data, focusing on a key generating mechanism of educational inequality: status signals produced by different college credentials which are exacerbated and evaluated by the ways we rank, sort, and categorize universities. Although the importance of college on life outcomes and analyses of differences based on qualitative dimensions of higher education are well-documented in the stratification literature, prior work is limited on two fronts: First, stratification research typically assumes that the role of education on social status is fully or largely mediated by the acquisition of occupational or income status; in other words, education is seen as a means to an end for social status, rather than an end in itself. Second, education research on college differences and educational status is reliant on opaque and imprecise measurements such as mainstream college ranking lists. My ongoing dissertation projects center (higher) education as a key source of status inequality to establish their theoretical and empirical importance, which is followed by the identification, production, comparison, and evaluation of different conceptions, operationalizations, and measurements of college status and prestige to discuss how these differences change our theoretical understanding(s) of the relationship between college status and later life outcomes. As such, my dissertation reconceptualizes and reprioritizes the role of education in social stratification: As (higher) education and credentials are now powerful and consequential markers of status, social status is consolidated and ossified much earlier in the life course than previously appreciated, which requires an updated framework for studies and solutions of social inequality.
